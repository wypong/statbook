    Section 13.2 The Spearman Test of Rank Correlation

    The ordinary correlation coefficient \(r\) measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient, written \(r_s\text{,}\) measures the strength and direction of a monotone relationship after the data values are replaced by ranks.
    🔗
    A relationship is monotone if it mostly moves in one direction. A positive monotone relationship tends to go up as \(x\) increases. A negative monotone relationship tends to go down as \(x\) increases. It does not have to be a straight line. So the Spearman test is about monotonicity, not linearity.
    🔗
    To compute \(r_s\text{,}\) rank the \(x\)-values from smallest to largest and rank the \(y\)-values from smallest to largest. Then compute the ordinary Pearson correlation coefficient using those ranks instead of the original data. If there are no ties, there is a shortcut formula. Let \(R_i\) be the rank of \(x_i\text{,}\) let \(S_i\) be the rank of \(y_i\text{,}\) and let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} d_i=R_i-S_i. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r_s=1-\frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}. \end{equation*}
    The denominator is important. The common no-ties formula has \(n(n^2-1)\) in the denominator, which is the same as \(n(n-1)(n+1)\text{.}\) A formula with only \(n(n-1)\) is missing the factor \(n+1\) and will not give the Spearman correlation coefficient.
    🔗
    Here is where the shortcut formula comes from. With no ties, both rank lists are just rearrangements of \(1,2,\ldots,n\text{,}\) so they have the same mean
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \overline{R}=\overline{S}=\frac{n+1}{2}. \end{equation*}
    The ordinary correlation of the ranks is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r_s= \frac{\sum (R_i-\overline{R})(S_i-\overline{S})} {\sqrt{\sum (R_i-\overline{R})^2}\sqrt{\sum (S_i-\overline{S})^2}}. \end{equation*}
    Because both rank lists have the same spread,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum (R_i-\overline{R})^2 = \sum (S_i-\overline{S})^2 = \frac{n(n^2-1)}{12}. \end{equation*}
    Now expand the squared rank differences:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum d_i^2 = \sum\left((R_i-\overline{R})-(S_i-\overline{S})\right)^2. \end{equation*}
    After expanding and collecting terms,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum d_i^2 = 2\sum (R_i-\overline{R})^2 -2\sum (R_i-\overline{R})(S_i-\overline{S}). \end{equation*}
    Solving this for the cross-product sum and dividing by \(\sum (R_i-\overline{R})^2=n(n^2-1)/12\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r_s=1-\frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}. \end{equation*}
    If there are tied values, assign tied observations their average rank and compute the ordinary correlation of the two rank columns. The shortcut formula is meant for the no-ties case.
    🔗

    Example 13.2.2. Computing Spearman Rank Correlation.

    Six students report hours studied and quiz scores.
    🔗
    Here \(\sum d^2=4\) and \(n=6\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} r_s=1-\frac{6(4)}{6(6^2-1)} =1-\frac{24}{210} \approx 0.886. \end{equation*}
    The rank correlation is strongly positive. Students who studied more tended to have higher quiz scores, though not in perfect rank order.
    🔗
    🔗
    The Spearman test usually starts with
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:\rho_s=0, \end{equation*}
    meaning no monotone association in the population. The alternative may be positive, negative, or two-sided. Small samples can be handled with exact rank-permutation methods. For large samples, a useful approximation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Z=r_s\sqrt{n-1}\approx N(0,1) \end{equation*}
    under \(H_0\text{.}\) This approximation has a concrete reason. Under the null hypothesis of no rank association, once the \(x\)-ranks are fixed, the \(y\)-ranks are essentially a random permutation of \(1,2,\ldots,n\text{.}\) In that random-permutation model, the Spearman statistic has mean 0 and variance
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{Var}(r_s)=\frac{1}{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    So multiplying by \(\sqrt{n-1}\) makes the variance equal to 1. As \(n\) grows, the permutation distribution of this standardized rank correlation becomes close to the standard normal distribution. That is why \(r_s\sqrt{n-1}\) can be treated as approximately standard normal for a large-sample test.
    🔗

    Example 13.2.4. A Large-Sample Spearman Test.

    A researcher studies whether stress rank and sleep-loss rank tend to move together for \(n=20\) students. The Spearman rank correlation is \(r_s=0.70\text{.}\) Test for positive monotone association.
    🔗
    The hypotheses are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:\rho_s=0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad H_a:\rho_s>0. \end{equation*}
    The large-sample statistic is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=r_s\sqrt{n-1}=0.70\sqrt{19}\approx 3.05. \end{equation*}
    The right-tailed p-value is about \(P(Z\ge 3.05)\approx 0.001\text{.}\) The data give strong evidence of a positive monotone association: students with higher stress ranks tend to have higher sleep-loss ranks.
    🔗

    Activity 13.2.1. Rank Correlation Card Sort.

    Practice reading monotone association without worrying about the original measurement units.
    🔗

    (a)

    Make two columns of paired numerical data with 8 observations. Rank each column from smallest to largest.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Shuffle only the second rank column until the pattern looks strongly positive, strongly negative, and then close to unrelated. For each arrangement, compute or estimate \(r_s\text{.}\)
