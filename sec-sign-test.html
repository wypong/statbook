    Section 13.1 The Sign Test

    The sign test is easy to describe because it ignores almost everything except whether each observation is above or below a hypothesized value. For a one-sample problem, the null hypothesis has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:M=M_0, \end{equation*}
    where \(M\) is the population median. This is the first important point: the sign test is a test about a median, not a test about a mean. A few extremely large or small observations can pull a mean around, but the sign test only asks how many observations fall on each side of \(M_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose \(H_0:M=M_0\) is true and the population is continuous, so ties at exactly \(M_0\) are unlikely. Then a randomly selected observation is just as likely to be above \(M_0\) as below it:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(X>M_0)=P(X<M_0)=\frac{1}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Let \(S\) be the number of observations above \(M_0\text{,}\) after throwing out any observations exactly equal to \(M_0\text{.}\) If there are \(n\) non-tied observations, then under the null hypothesis
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S\sim B\left(n,\frac{1}{2}\right). \end{equation*}
    So the sign test is a particular case of a binomial proportion test with \(p=1/2\text{.}\) The “success” is simply being on one chosen side of the hypothesized median.
    🔗
    The alternative hypothesis tells us which tail of the binomial distribution to use.
    🔗
    • For \(H_a:M>M_0\text{,}\) large values of \(S\) count against \(H_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • For \(H_a:M<M_0\text{,}\) small values of \(S\) count against \(H_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • For \(H_a:M\ne M_0\text{,}\) values of \(S\) far from \(n/2\) in either direction count against \(H_0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    For small samples, we base the rejection decision directly on the binomial distribution. For large samples, we use the normal approximation to the binomial distribution. Since \(p=1/2\text{,}\) the null mean and standard deviation of \(S\) are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mu_S=\frac{n}{2} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sigma_S=\sqrt{n\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)} =\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2}. \end{equation*}
    Thus the large-sample sign-test statistic is approximately
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=\frac{S-n/2}{\sqrt{n}/2}. \end{equation*}
    A continuity correction is often used when converting a binomial tail to a normal tail, but the main idea is the same: the observed number of plus signs is compared with what a fair \(p=1/2\) binomial model predicts.
    🔗

    Example 13.1.1. An Exact One-Sample Sign Test.

    A student group claims that the median time to find parking on campus is more than 10 minutes. A random sample of 12 students gives parking times where 10 are above 10 minutes and 2 are below 10 minutes. There are no ties at exactly 10 minutes.
    🔗
    We test
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:M=10 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:M>10. \end{equation*}
    Let \(S\) be the number of times above 10 minutes. Under \(H_0\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S\sim B\left(12,\frac{1}{2}\right). \end{equation*}
    Since the alternative is right-tailed, the p-value is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} P(S\ge 10) &=P(S=10)+P(S=11)+P(S=12)\\ &=\frac{\binom{12}{10}+\binom{12}{11}+\binom{12}{12}}{2^{12}}\\ &=\frac{79}{4096}\approx 0.019. \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    At the 5% significance level, we reject \(H_0\text{.}\) The sample gives evidence that the median parking time is more than 10 minutes.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 13.1.2. A Large-Sample Sign Test.

    A city wants to know whether the median commute time is greater than 30 minutes. In a random sample of 64 commuters, 43 report commute times above 30 minutes, 21 report commute times below 30 minutes, and nobody reports exactly 30 minutes.
    🔗
    Under \(H_0:M=30\text{,}\) the number above 30 minutes has approximately mean \(64/2=32\) and standard deviation \(\sqrt{64}/2=4\text{.}\) The z statistic is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z=\frac{43-32}{4}=2.75. \end{equation*}
    For the right-tailed alternative \(H_a:M>30\text{,}\) the p-value is about \(P(Z\ge 2.75)\approx 0.003\text{.}\) The data give strong evidence that the median commute time is greater than 30 minutes.
    🔗
    🔗
    The sign test also works naturally for paired data. For a paired-sample sign test, first compute the paired differences
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} d_i=\text{after}_i-\text{before}_i \end{equation*}
    or whatever direction makes sense in context. Then ignore the zero differences and count the signs of the nonzero differences. The null hypothesis is about the median difference:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:M_d=0. \end{equation*}
    If the treatment has no median effect, positive and negative differences should be equally likely, so the number of positive differences again follows \(B(n,1/2)\) under the null hypothesis.
    🔗

    Example 13.1.3. A Paired-Sample Sign Test.

    Ten students take a short statistics quiz before and after a review session. Let \(d=\text{after}-\text{before}\text{.}\) The signs of the differences are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} +,\ +,\ -,\ +,\ +,\ +,\ -,\ +,\ +,\ +. \end{equation*}
    There are 8 positive differences and 2 negative differences. To test whether the review session tends to improve scores, use
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:M_d=0 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_a:M_d>0. \end{equation*}
    Under \(H_0\text{,}\) the number of positive signs is \(S\sim B(10,1/2)\text{.}\) The p-value is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} P(S\ge 8)=\frac{\binom{10}{8}+\binom{10}{9}+\binom{10}{10}}{2^{10}} =\frac{56}{1024}\approx 0.055. \end{equation*}
    At \(\alpha=0.05\text{,}\) this is close but not quite small enough to reject \(H_0\text{.}\) The sign pattern suggests improvement, but the evidence is not quite strong enough at the 5% level.
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 13.1.1. Human Sign Test.

    Use a quick class data set to see why the sign test is a binomial test in disguise.
    🔗

    (a)

    Choose a claim about a median that the class can check quickly, such as “the median commute time to campus is 20 minutes.” Each student records whether their value is above, below, or exactly equal to the claimed median.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Throw out any ties. Count the number above the claimed median and the number below it.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Explain why the number above the claimed median should follow \(B(n,1/2)\) if the claim is true.
