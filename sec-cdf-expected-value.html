    Section 7.2 Properties of Expectation and Variance

    The formulas in the previous section tell us how to compute expectation and variance. Now we collect a few properties that are useful much more broadly. These are not specific to discrete random variables.
    🔗
    Expectation is linear. For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) and constants \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\)
    🔗
    1. Expectation is additive:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \E(X+Y)=\E(X)+\E(Y). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Expectation respects scaling
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \E(aX)=a\E(X). \end{equation*}
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    These properties are intuitive: the average of sums is the sum of the averages, and adding or multiplying every value changes the average in the same way. Note also that since the probabilities sum to 1,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \E(1)=\sum_{x\in R_X}1P(X=x)=1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Consequently, it follows from the scaling property of expectation that for any constant \(a\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \E(a) = \E(a1) = a\E(1) =a. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    This certainly agrees with one instinct that the average of a constant is that constant itself.
    🔗
    For variance, using the linearity of expectation, we have
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \var(aX) & = \E(aX)^2 - (\E(aX))^2 \\ & = \E(a^2 X^2) - (a\E(X))^2 \\ & = a^2 \E(X^2) - a^2(\E(X))^2 = a^2\var(X). \end{align*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \var(X+Y) & = \E(X+Y)^2 - (\E(X+Y))^2\\ & = \E(X^2 + 2XY +Y^2) - (\E(X))^2 - 2\E(X)\E(Y) - (\E(Y))^2\\ & = \var(X) + \var(Y) + 2\cov(X,Y) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Thus, if \(X\) and \(Y\) are uncorrelated, i.e. \(\cov(X,Y) = 0\text{,}\) in particular when \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \var(X+Y)=\var(X)+\var(Y). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note also that for any constant \(a\text{,}\) since \(a = \E(a)\text{,}\) \(\cov(X,a) = \E(X-\E(X))(a - \E(a)) = 0\text{.}\) In particular, \(\var(a) = \cov(a,a) = 0\text{.}\) Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \var(X+a) = \var(X) + \var(a) + 2\cov(X,a) = \var(X). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Remember, expectation always adds, but variance adds only under extra conditions such as independence.
    🔗

    Example 7.2.1. Using the Properties.

    Suppose \(\E(X)=5\text{,}\) \(\var(X)=4\text{,}\) \(\E(Y)=2\text{,}\) and \(\var(Y)=9\text{.}\) Assume \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.
    🔗
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \E(3X-1)=3\E(X)-1=3(5)-1=14, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \var(3X-1)=3^2\var(X)=9(4)=36, \end{equation*}
    🔗
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \var(X+Y)=\var(X)+\var(Y)=4+9=13. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    These properties are handy when units change. For example, converting from meters to centimeters multiplies both the expected value and the standard deviation by 100, but it multiplies the variance by \(100^2\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Activity 7.2.2. Stretch the Score.

    See how adding and multiplying a random variable change its mean and spread.
    🔗

    (a)

    Suppose a game score has \(\E(X)=6\) and \(\var(X)=4\text{.}\) Find \(\E(3X-2)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(\var(3X-2)\) and the standard deviation of \(3X-2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    In one sentence, explain why shifting changes the mean but not the spread, while multiplying changes both.

    Activity 7.2.3. Two Games, One Total.

    Combine two independent random variables and see how expectation and variance behave.
    🔗

    (a)

    Suppose game A gives a random score \(X\) with \(\E(X)=5\) and \(\var(X)=1\text{.}\) Game B gives a random score \(Y\) with \(\E(Y)=7\) and \(\var(Y)=9\text{.}\) Assuming the games are independent, find \(\E(X+Y)\) and \(\var(X+Y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Find \(\E(2X-Y)\) and \(\var(2X-Y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Which game contributes more to the variability of the total, and why?
