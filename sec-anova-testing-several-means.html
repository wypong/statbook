    Section 12.2 Testing Several Means

    A one-way ANOVA test compares several population means using one categorical explanatory variable. The groups might be teaching methods, fertilizer types, treatment doses, or class sections.
    🔗
    The hypotheses are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:\mu_1=\mu_2=\cdots=\mu_m \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_a:\text{at least one population mean is different.} \end{equation*}
    The alternative does not say that every mean is different from every other mean. It only says that the means are not all equal.
    🔗
    The test statistic is built from the variation split in Section 12.1. The mean square between is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} MSB=\frac{nB}{m-1}, \end{equation*}
    and the mean square within is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} MSW=\frac{W}{m(n-1)}. \end{equation*}
    The ANOVA F statistic is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F=\frac{MSB}{MSW} = \frac{nB/(m-1)}{W/[m(n-1)]}. \end{equation*}
    Large values of \(F\) count against \(H_0\text{.}\) A large numerator means the group means are spread out. A small denominator means the observations inside each group are fairly tight. That combination makes the differences among the group means look too large to blame on ordinary within-group noise.
    🔗
    To run the test at significance level \(\alpha\text{,}\) compare the observed statistic with a right-tail critical value from Appendix F:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F_{\alpha}\left(m-1,\ m(n-1)\right). \end{equation*}
    Equivalently, compute the right-tail p-value. Reject \(H_0\) when the p-value is less than \(\alpha\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 12.2.2. Testing Three Teaching Methods.

    Use the teaching-method data from Example 12.1.2. We want to test whether the three population mean score gains are equal.
    🔗
    The hypotheses are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} H_0:\mu_A=\mu_B=\mu_C \qquad\text{and}\qquad H_a:\text{at least one mean differs.} \end{equation*}
    From the earlier calculation, \(m=3\text{,}\) \(n=4\text{,}\) \(B\approx 25.167\text{,}\) and \(W=12\text{.}\) Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} MSB=\frac{nB}{m-1} = \frac{4(25.167)}{2} \approx 50.333, \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} MSW=\frac{W}{m(n-1)} = \frac{12}{3(3)} = 1.333. \end{equation*}
    The F statistic is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F=\frac{50.333}{1.333}\approx 37.75. \end{equation*}
    The degrees of freedom are \(m-1=2\) and \(m(n-1)=9\text{.}\) At \(\alpha=0.05\text{,}\) the F-table critical value is about \(F_{0.05}(2,9)=4.256\text{.}\) Since \(37.75>4.256\text{,}\) we reject \(H_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The data give strong evidence that the three teaching methods do not all have the same population mean score gain. This conclusion does not, by itself, identify exactly which methods differ. For that, we would need follow-up comparisons.
    🔗
    🔗
    The usual conditions for one-way ANOVA are:
    🔗
    • The observations are independent, and the groups are formed by random sampling or random assignment.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Each group population is approximately normal, especially when the sample sizes are small.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The group populations have a common variance \(\sigma^2\text{.}\) This is the equal-variance assumption.
      🔗
      🔗
    The equal-variance condition explains why the denominator \(MSW\) is so important. If the groups all have the same population variance, then the within-group variation gives a pooled estimate of that common variance. The numerator \(MSB\) should estimate the same variance when \(H_0\) is true, but it tends to become much larger when the group means are genuinely different.
    🔗
