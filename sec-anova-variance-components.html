    Section 12.1 Components of Variation

    Suppose we have \(m\) groups and \(n\) observations in each group. Write \(x_{ij}\) for observation \(j\) in group \(i\text{.}\) The group mean is \(\overline{x}_{i\cdot}\text{,}\) and the grand mean of all \(mn\) observations is \(\overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    ANOVA begins by splitting total variation into two pieces. The total variation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(x_{ij}-\overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^2. \end{equation*}
    The between-group variation measures how far the group means are from the grand mean:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} B=\sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\overline{x}_{i\cdot}-\overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^2. \end{equation*}
    The within-group variation measures how far observations are from their own group mean:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} W=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(x_{ij}-\overline{x}_{i\cdot}\right)^2. \end{equation*}
    With this shorthand, the balanced one-way ANOVA identity is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T=nB+W. \end{equation*}
    The factor \(n\) appears because each group mean represents \(n\) observations. If a group mean is 4 units above the grand mean, then all \(n\) observations in that group inherit that 4-unit group-level offset before we even look at their individual wiggles around the group mean.
    🔗
    The identity \(T=nB+W\) is not a statistical approximation. It is algebra. For every observation, write its distance from the grand mean as a two-step trip:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x_{ij}-\overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot} = \left(\overline{x}_{i\cdot}-\overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot}\right) + \left(x_{ij}-\overline{x}_{i\cdot}\right). \end{equation*}
    The first term is the group mean’s distance from the grand mean. The second term is the observation’s distance from its group mean. When we square and sum over all observations, the cross-product terms disappear because, within each group, deviations from the group mean sum to 0:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{ij}-\overline{x}_{i\cdot}\right)=0. \end{equation*}
    What remains is the between part counted \(n\) times, plus the within part:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(x_{ij}-\overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^2 = n\sum_{i=1}^{m} \left(\overline{x}_{i\cdot}-\overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot}\right)^2 + \sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(x_{ij}-\overline{x}_{i\cdot}\right)^2. \end{equation*}

    Example 12.1.2. Splitting Total Variation.

    Three teaching methods are compared using quiz-score gains. Each method is used with four students.
    🔗
    The grand mean is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \overline{x}_{\cdot\cdot} = \frac{6+8+7+9+10+11+9+10+13+15+14+16}{12} = 10.667. \end{equation*}
    The between-group part is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} B=(7.5-10.667)^2+(10-10.667)^2+(14.5-10.667)^2 \approx 25.167. \end{equation*}
    Since each group has \(n=4\) observations, the between contribution to total variation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} nB=4(25.167)\approx 100.667. \end{equation*}
    The within-group variation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} W=5+2+5=12. \end{equation*}
    So the total variation should be
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T=nB+W\approx 100.667+12=112.667. \end{equation*}
    Most of the variation here comes from the group means being far apart, not from large spread inside the groups.
    🔗
    🔗
    The connection with the F distribution comes from comparing two independent estimates of the same population variance. Recall the definition: if \(U\) and \(V\) are independent chi-square random variables with \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) degrees of freedom, then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{U/d_1}{V/d_2}\sim F(d_1,d_2). \end{equation*}
    Under the ANOVA null hypothesis that all group means are equal, and assuming normal populations with a common variance \(\sigma^2\text{,}\) the two scaled variation pieces behave like independent chi-square random variables:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{nB}{\sigma^2}\sim \chi^2_{m-1} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{W}{\sigma^2}\sim \chi^2_{m(n-1)}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore the ratio
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F= \frac{nB/(m-1)}{W/[m(n-1)]} \end{equation*}
    has the distribution
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} F\sim F\left(m-1,\ m(n-1)\right) \end{equation*}
    when the null hypothesis is true. In words, the numerator is the between-group variation per between-group degree of freedom, and the denominator is the within-group variation per within-group degree of freedom. If the group means are truly equal, those two quantities should be estimating the same common variance \(\sigma^2\text{,}\) so their ratio should usually be near 1.
    🔗

    Activity 12.1.1. Where Did the Variation Go?

    Use a small data set to see the ANOVA split by hand.
    🔗

    (a)

    In groups, make three small samples of size 4. Try to make one data set where the three group means are close together, and another where the group means are far apart but the within-group spreads are similar.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    For each data set, compute the group means, the grand mean, \(B\text{,}\) \(W\text{,}\) and \(nB+W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Which data set has the larger between-group component? Which has the larger within-group component?
